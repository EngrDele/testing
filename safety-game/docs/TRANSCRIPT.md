**Yes, Tailscale can give you a "just send the link" setup.** Its **Funnel** feature turns a computer you own into a public web address like `https://your-pc.your-name.ts.net`. **How it works for you:**
1. Install Tailscale on an office PC or laptop and sign in.
2. Put the game files in a folder and run a small web server for that folder.
3. Turn on Funnel, and Tailscale gives you the public link.
4. Send the link on WhatsApp or email, or put it on a QR code at the gate.

**Good points:**
- Quick to set up, free, and you control everything on your own machine.
- Because the game runs from your own PC, **results can be saved straight onto that PC**, for example into a spreadsheet file for HSE. Remote operators' results would reach you, which the claude.ai link can't do for people outside your organization.

**Weak points:**
- **The computer must stay on and online 24/7.** If it sleeps, the power goes off or the internet drops, the link stops working. In Lagos that means a UPS, or keeping it on the generator.
- **Speed depends on your office upload speed.** Every player downloads about 1 MB from your machine. That's fine for a few people at once; a full intake of 50 at the same time could be slow.
- **No published bandwidth limit.** Tailscale says traffic is limited but doesn't say by how much. Some users never hit it, while others won't use it for serious work because the limit isn't stated.
- It suits **demos, pilots and small groups**, not a company-wide rollout.

**Claude:**

I checked [tailscale/tailcat](https://github.com/tailscale/tailcat). It's a different tool from what I described, and **it isn't a good fit for "just send the link."**

**What tailcat is:** "like netcat, but over Tailscale's data plane." It makes an **encrypted tunnel between two machines that both run tailcat**. You don't need a Tailscale account or admin rights, which is nice. Your PC prints an address, and the other person runs tailcat on their side to connect to it.

**Why it doesn't suit remote operators:**
1. **The operator has to install and run tailcat too.** There's no normal web link that a contractor can tap on WhatsApp and play.
2. **Its "serve files" feature isn't a website.** It uses SFTP, a file-transfer protocol, not the web protocol browsers use, so it can't host the game page directly. You could run a web server and forward its port, but each player would still need tailcat on their phone or laptop to reach it.
3. **The address is a secret password.** Anyone who gets it can connect, so posting it in a group chat or on a QR code is risky.
4. **It's very new and experimental.** It was open-sourced in August 2026, with "no API or CLI stability promises," and the free relays have no uptime guarantee.
5. **Your computer must stay on**, the same as any self-hosted option.

**Where tailcat is useful:** connecting two of *your own* machines, for example sending the game files from your laptop to an office PC, or letting a colleague reach a test version privately.

**If you want "send a link, they tap it, it opens in the browser":**

| Option | What the operator does |
|---|---|
| **Tailscale Funnel** (same company, different feature) | Taps a normal `https://…ts.net` link. Nothing to install, but your PC must stay on. |
| **Cloudflare Pages** (my recommendation) | Taps a normal link. Nothing to install, no PC to keep running, and fast in Nigeria. |
| claude.ai link (current) | Taps the link. Works for staff in your organization, but results from outside people aren't saved. |

Yes, **VS Code can do this, and it's a good choice for testing.** Its Port Forwarding feature gives you a public `https://…devtunnels.ms` link that anyone can open in a browser on any phone or computer, in any company, with nothing to install.

## How to do it (about 5 minutes)

1. **Get the game onto your computer.** On GitHub, open `EngrDele/testing`, switch to the branch `claude/lagos-safety-game-research-cq4tb4`, and choose **Code → Download ZIP**. Unzip it.
2. **Open the folder in VS Code:** File → Open Folder → choose the unzipped folder.
3. **Start a small web server.** Install the **Live Server** extension, right-click `safety-game/index.html` and choose **"Open with Live Server"**. It usually runs on port **5500**.
   - Or, if Python is installed, open the Terminal and type `python -m http.server 8080`.
4. **Make it public:**
   - Open the **Ports** tab at the bottom of VS Code. If you can't see it, use View → Open View → Ports.
   - Click **Forward a Port**, type **5500** (or 8080), and sign in with your **GitHub or Microsoft** account when asked.
   - **Right-click the port → Port Visibility → Public.** It's private by default, and private links only work for you.
5. **Copy the link.** Hover over the Forwarded Address and copy it. Add the game path to the end, for example:
   `https://abc123-5500.uks1.devtunnels.ms/safety-game/index.html`
6. **Send it on WhatsApp or email.** Anyone can open it; no Claude account is needed.

## Things to know
- **It only works while your computer is on, VS Code is open and the server is running.** If the PC sleeps, the power goes off or you close VS Code, the link stops.
- **Anyone with the link can open it**, so share it only with your testers.
- **Microsoft limits bandwidth and usage** on these tunnels without publishing fixed numbers. That's fine for testing with a few people, not for a company-wide rollout.
- People may see a **one-time "developer tunnel" warning page** with a Continue button the first time they open the link. Tell testers that's expected.
- The link can change if you remove and re-add the port.
- **Results:** players' results stay on their own phones, because there's no HSE store behind this link.

Honestly: **yes, the core idea is good, and worth pursuing. But it isn't great yet, and how far it goes depends less on the game than on the business around it.**

## What's genuinely strong
- **The problem is real.** "Watch the video, tick the paper" is everywhere, everyone knows it doesn't work, and HSE managers would admit that privately. You don't have to convince anyone the problem exists.
- **Site-specific is the key insight.** Generic safety videos exist everywhere. Training on *this compound's* gate, generator house and forklift lane is what makes it relevant and memorable.

- **The Nigerian context fits.** Browser-based, runs on cheap Android phones, works offline, and the content is local (NEPA, okada, gen fumes, "Oga said do it"). Foreign VR training products don't do this and are far too expensive for most Nigerian companies.
- **Low cost to run.** No headsets, no installs, no training room.

## Where I'd be cautious
- **It's not a new idea globally.** VR and game-based safety inductions already exist abroad, mostly sold to oil and gas and mining. Your edge is **local, affordable and mobile**, not the concept itself. That's still a real opening, but say it that way to investors and clients.
- **The hard part is building each client's site, not the game.** Every company needs its own compound modelled, its own hazards and its own rules. If each one takes weeks of custom work, it won't scale or make money. The business works only if you can turn a site walk, photos and a floor plan into a playable site in days. That pipeline is the real product.
- **Don't confuse it with Lagos Life.** Lagos Life went viral because millions of individuals played for fun. This is B2B: HSE managers, procurement and slow decisions. Growth will come from sales and pilots, not virality.
- **The evidence is still thin.** Studies suggest games beat videos for retention, but they're small. Clients, especially big ones, will ask "does it reduce incidents?" You need your own pilot numbers.
- **The visuals will always be compared to real games.** You've seen that already. It doesn't need to look like GTA, but it does need to look professional and run well on a ₦100k phone. That's a balance to manage.
- **It shouldn't replace the physical walk-round.** Its strength is preparing people before they set foot on site. Pitch it as "better induction", not "no more induction".

## What would make it great
1. **Run one real pilot** with a company you know, such as a factory, warehouse or construction site. Compare the old induction with the game on a 30-day hazard-spotting test and on near-miss reporting. Real numbers from one site are worth more than any feature.
2. **Build the "site builder"** so a new client's compound can be set up quickly from a template plus their photos and hazards.
3. **Sell it as a service:** "We map your site, build your induction, and give you the dashboard," for a setup fee plus a monthly charge per site. Start with sectors where induction is mandatory and incidents are costly: oil and gas service companies, manufacturing, construction and logistics.
4. **Get an endorsement:** the Lagos State Safety Commission, IOSH trainers or an insurer would add a lot of credibility.

**My verdict:** a solid idea with a real market, especially in Nigeria and West Africa, and a clear advantage over expensive foreign alternatives. The prototype already shows what it can be. It becomes great if you prove it works on one real site and find a cheap, repeatable way to build each new site. I'd spend your next effort on a pilot, not more features.

**Claude:**

For ages 3 to 6, I'd add a separate **"Little Stars"** world rather than make the existing kids games easier. Most children this age can't read, have short attention spans and are still learning to use a touchscreen, so the game has to work differently.

## How it should play

| Ages 6–12 games now | Little Stars (3–6) |
|---|---|
| Reading questions | **No reading.** Everything is spoken aloud in English or Pidgin, with pictures for the answers |
| Walk with joystick and camera | **Tap only.** A guide (Mummy or a cartoon friend) walks for the child, and the child taps big glowing objects |
| Hearts you can lose | **No losing.** A wrong tap gets a gentle "Ouch! That's hot. Let's try again", then a hint |
| 10–15 minutes | **3–5 minutes**, 4–5 short scenes |
| Certificate | **Stickers and a song** at the end, plus a "Show Mummy/Daddy" screen with a tip for the parent |
| 3D world walking | 3D room seen from above, bright colours, big objects, slow movement |

Parents play alongside, so each scene ends with a one-line tip for the adult, for example "Keep medicines locked up high".

## Game ideas, most important first

1. **"Hot! Ouch!" in the kitchen.** Tap things that can burn: the pot on the stove, the iron, the hot tea, the kerosene lamp. Burns from cooking pots and hot water are a common injury for toddlers in Nigerian homes.
2. **Water safety at home.** An open bucket or drum of water, the well, the bath. The rule is "Never play near water without a big person". Toddlers can drown in a bucket, which matters where homes store water.
3. **Medicine is not sweets.** Sort sweets from tablets and syrups, and bleach or kerosene in a drink bottle from real drinks. "Only Mummy gives medicine." Kerosene poisoning from drink bottles is a known risk for children.
4. **Don't touch the socket.** Sockets, naked wires, the generator, and a phone charging. "Electricity bites!"
5. **Hold hands at the road.** Mummy says "Stop, look, hold my hand", and the child taps "stop" when the danfo comes. No free crossing at this age.
6. **Fire and smoke.** Smoke appears, so get low, go out and call a big person. Gas smell means "Tell Mummy, don't touch the switch".
7. **My name and Mummy's number.** A sing-along to learn their full name, a parent's name and phone number, and "If I'm lost, I stand still and find a market mummy or a police."
8. **My body is mine.** A very gentle "say no, run, tell" rule about private parts and secrets, worded for small children and checked by a child-protection professional before it goes in.
9. **Clean hands.** Wash hands before eating and after the toilet, in a tap-and-scrub mini-game.
10. **Car and keke seat.** Sit inside, buckle up, keep hands and head inside.

**How it plays**

**The ten scenes**
1. **Hot! Ouch!** Tap the pot, the iron, the hot tea and the lamp.
2. **Water at home:** an open bucket or drum, an uncovered well, bath water.
3. **Medicine is not sweets:** sort food from tablets, syrup, bleach, and kerosene in a drink bottle.
4. **Electricity bites:** sockets, naked wires, the generator, a charging phone.
5. **Hold hands at the road:** hold Mummy's hand, stop, look, wait for the danfo to pass, then cross at the zebra.
6. **Fire and smoke:** get low and go out, tell a big person, what to do if you smell gas, and stop, drop and roll.
7. **My name and Mummy's number:** a sing-along, then choosing the right helper if lost in the market.
8. **My body is mine:** "No, go away, tell Mummy", and good surprises versus bad secrets.
9. **Clean hands:** wet, soap, scrub until the germs are gone, rinse, dry, and when to wash.
10. **Car and keke seat:** sit in the back seat, buckle up, hands inside the keke.
